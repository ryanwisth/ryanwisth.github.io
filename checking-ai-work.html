Checking AI · three tools in use

Making AI work easy to check

One idea, used in three tools: the AI suggests, plain code enforces the rules, and a second check tries to prove the first one wrong.

Problem
AI writing sounds right and is still wrong in places, and reading it will not show you where. Asking the AI to check itself just gives a second confident answer, with no record behind it.
Result
Three tools that turn AI output into checked work with a written record. The fact-checker checked and recorded 517 statements in one AI-written report. In the research tool, “skeptic” AI helpers, told to hunt for mistakes, still rejected 3 of 147 statements that had passed a careful check. The spending guard now makes every AI helper use the light setup; before, 482 of 662 had used the heavy one. The fact-checker and the spending guard have been used daily since October 2026. The research tool ran three rounds in September 2026.

The problem

Asking the AI to “double-check” gives a second confident answer, with no record of what was checked or how.

Written rules do not fix this. In my own work the AI agreed to the rules, then broke them an hour later. The fix had to be built into code, where a rule cannot be forgotten.

Decisions I made

  • Research: genealogy-evidence-pipeline. Every statement must name and quote an original record. A small free program drops anything that breaks the rules before any AI time is spent. Then a skeptic AI tries to disprove the rest.
  • Reports: claim-ledger. Every point in a long report gets a fixed ID, a statement that can be tested, what would prove it wrong, quoted evidence and a verdict. Code enforces this form: one incomplete entry rejects the whole batch, so no check is ever half-done.
  • Cost: claude-code-token-guard. Checks that run automatically block a wasteful AI action the moment it is tried, and the message tells the AI what to do instead.

Each tool also has its own page: the records-only family tree, the fact-checking routine and the spending rules.

How it was tested

  • Research. The public copy runs start to finish without the internet, on a made-up family of 420 people. In that run the free filter kept 12 of 16 statements and dropped 4 before any skeptic review.
  • Reports. Running the first report’s 546 entries back through the tool gives the same result as the review done by hand. That review also shows where the value is: batches 1 to 9 found problems in 27% of first checks, while batches 10 to 29 found 7% and took 73% of the work. So the tool says when more checking stops paying off.
  • Cost. Each check targets a waste measured in my own usage logs, and each was confirmed by the next week’s numbers.

Limits

  • The same AI writes and checks, so the fact-checker marks any verdict that rests on the project’s own notes for a person to verify.
  • The public research demo uses a made-up family of 420 people.
