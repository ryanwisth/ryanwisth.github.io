Case study: research honest enough to fail

A study built so it could fail

A research tool that converts pinball table layouts, measures their geometry, plays them with simulated players at four skill levels, and tests whether any of that predicts how much people enjoy a table. The answer was no, and the write-up says so.

The problem

The goal was to score an original table design before anyone had played it: learn what separates well-rated tables from the rest, then apply that to the new design.

The easy trap in a study like this is to try many measures and report the ones that happen to fit.

What I directed

I set the research question, the plan and its pass/fail gates, chose between options at each decision point, and checked the results. The agents wrote the code and ran the experiments.

  • Hypotheses before results. Designer rules of thumb were written down with predicted directions, a fixed test and a fixed verdict rule, then hashed, before any measure was compared with a rating.
  • One common board format. The simulator never sees a source file, so an original design can be measured exactly like a converted commercial table.
  • Port the physics, do not invent it. A hand-tuned flipper was replaced by a port of an established simulator's flipper model and checked against an independent port.
  • Controls in every model. Year and maker are always included, so era and brand are not mistaken for "fun".
  • Trust only what holds up. The expert bot did not reliably beat the intermediate one, so every hypothesis uses the intermediate bot and the expert-based test is marked low-trust.

How the results were verified

  • Every rating model was judged by leave-one-out cross-validation against the simple baseline of predicting the mean.
  • Each hypothesis was tested with bootstrap intervals on two separate measures of success: public rating and production run.
  • The hashes of the hypothesis files are stored with the results, so an edit after the fact shows.

The outcome

The hypothesis failed. The best model's error was 0.425 against 0.448 for guessing the mean, and year, maker and build flags alone did as well. Of the 9 rules, none passed as predicted, and two went the opposite way, about what chance gives.

The best reading is that ratings mostly reward theme, licence, rules depth, sound and code, which a 2D simulator cannot see. What remains useful is the instrument: a converter, a simulator and bots that can check a design for stuck balls, unmakeable ramps and drains down the middle.

Honest limits

  • The scorer is not a usable fun predictor, and nothing in the project claims it is.
  • The design-check use of the simulator was not built out. Ramps on converted tables are rarely made, and 18% of ramps could not be made by any aimed shot.
  • The published hypothesis files had a few non-substantive sentences and labels removed, so they no longer match the hashes recorded during the study; the README states both.
