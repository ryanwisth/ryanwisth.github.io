Giving the AI on my laptop tools to use

Six toolkits (in a standard format AI apps understand, called MCP) that give the AI running on my laptop what it lacks: live world news, market prices and company figures, a check of software parts for known security holes, a look-only study coach, and a reader that turns pictures of equations into text.

Problem
An AI on a laptop knows nothing after the date it was trained, and cannot look anything up. Public toolkits are built for big online AIs: one lists 90 tools and about 100k tokens of instructions (a token is a small chunk of text), enough to swamp a laptop AI before the chat starts.
Result
Six one-file toolkits give the laptop AI live data and local tools, with 15 or fewer tools each, against 90 in one public toolkit. A format change cut price-history replies from about 149 to about 48 characters per day. So five years of prices now fit under the 12,000-character limit, instead of cutting off after about 81 days. The company-data toolkit is in real use in a chat app on my laptop.

Read the script

15 tools or fewer per toolkit, where one public toolkit lists 90. Six small toolkits give the AI on a laptop tools it can use. Short tool lists, math done by code, and tools that only look, never change. A compact format cut each day of prices from about 149 to about 48 characters, so five years come back whole. Replies are limited to 12,000 characters, and any cut-off is announced. Empty data becomes a clear error, and errors come back in plain English. The company-data toolkit is in real use.

The problem

Data services publish their own toolkits, but they are built for big online AIs. One lists 90 tools and about 100k tokens of instructions. On a laptop, that costs minutes per new chat and most of what the AI can hold in mind at once.

So I built each toolkit here small and by hand: 15 or fewer tools, small default requests and limited replies, so a mid-sized laptop AI can pick the right tool and read the answer without drowning in it.

Decisions I made

  • Shape tools for the AI that will read them. Each tool list is short, each tool says when to use it, default requests are small, and tools that can only fail on the free plan are left out.
  • Do math in code, not in the AI. The comparison tool returns ranked percent changes, not raw prices, because math over long lists of numbers is exactly where small AIs go wrong.
  • Make empty data loud. A wrong stock symbol comes back from the data service as blanks, not an error. The toolkit turns that into a clear error, because an AI handed blanks tends to report them as data.
  • Measure before raising a limit. In the wordy format, the history tool hit the 12,000-character limit after about 81 days. The compact table format cut each day from about 149 to about 48 characters, and using fewer points over long spans lets five years come back whole, with the limit unchanged.
  • Look, never change. There are no tools to buy or sell anywhere, and the study coach leaves out everything that would fetch or answer a question.
  • Errors an AI can read. Errors come back in plain English, never as technical dumps, and a missing login returns a setup message instead of failing or freezing.

How it was tested

  • Company data: working, in real use. Tested with live data on September 18, 2026 and added to a chat app on my laptop.
  • Security check: working. Tested on November 13, 2026 with a test program.
  • Study coach: working. Tested on November 13, 2026 against the practice app with its 21 sample questions; all six tools returned data.
  • World news: built and self-tested. 15 tools listed, a missing or expired login returns the setup message, and a free self-test ran against the live service.

Limits

  • Look-only throughout: no tools to buy or sell, and service passwords (keys) stay on the laptop.
  • Free to reuse (MIT license); not tied to any data provider, and each service’s own terms apply.
